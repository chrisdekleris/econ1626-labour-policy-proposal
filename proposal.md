# Watching for the First Signal: A Trigger-Based Response to AI in the Australian Labour Market

**Prepared by:** Chris · ECON1626 Economics of Artificial Intelligence · Assessment 2

This brief advises the Deputy Secretary, Employment and Workforce, Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR 2026c), on extending the Office of the Chief Economist's AI monitoring framework before its quarterly updates resume in late 2026 (DEWR 2026a). It also informs the department's input to the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, which reports by 30 November 2026 (Parliament of Australia 2026).

## Executive Summary

## 1. Problem Definition and Context

Theory cannot sign AI's net effect on jobs: automation displaces workers from tasks, while productivity gains and new tasks pull labour back in (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019). In Australia, exposure is broad but automation risk is narrow. About 4% of workers are in occupations with high automation exposure and 79% in occupations with low exposure, with the risk concentrated in routine clerical and administrative roles (JSA 2025).

A worker's loss is not automatically a market failure (Berg 2026). The problem this brief addresses is mainly one of **adjustment and insurance**: skills can lose value faster than workers can retrain, and few workers can insure against that risk privately. A secondary **information** problem justifies public action on monitoring, because timely labour-market evidence is a public good that no single firm has reason to produce.

DEWR's framework has four gaps. It measures employment only, not wages or tasks within jobs; its survey cannot split occupational trends by age; it publishes nothing until late 2026 while the ABS reclassifies occupations (DEWR 2026a); and no response is attached to any result. Because technologies diffuse slowly, finding no effect yet is expected and offers little reassurance.

## 2. Analysis of Impacts

### 2.1 Employment

Unemployment was 4.5% in July 2026 (ABS 2026), but employment in the most-exposed fifth of occupations grew 5.6% between November 2022 and February 2026, against 9.5% in the least-exposed fifth. DEWR's model implies employment about 2% below trend for occupations one standard deviation above average exposure, although the result loses significance under two alternative exposure measures (DEWR 2026a). It is a signal to watch, not yet a finding.

### 2.2 Tasks and hiring

The first adjustment is likely to come through hiring. In US payroll data, employment of 22–25 year olds in highly exposed occupations sits about 19% below trend, through reduced hiring rather than separations, and mainly where AI automates tasks (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen 2026). AI lifted novice productivity by 34% against 14% on average (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond 2025), so it substitutes most closely for entry-level work, and firms can shrink graduate intake without dismissing anyone. Australia's hiring rate has fallen from 5.5% to 4.2% since November 2022, which DEWR attributes mainly to fewer job-to-job moves, and young people feel hiring slowdowns first. Youth employment has held up so far, but early administrative data show lower hiring among younger exposed workers (DEWR 2026a).

### 2.3 Wages

No wage effect has appeared. Danish data rule out earnings effects above 2% (Humlum & Vestergaard 2025), and US adjustment shows in employment rather than base pay (Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen 2026). Because wages are sticky (DEWR 2026a), a framework waiting for wage or unemployment signals will act late.

### 2.4 Productivity and industry structure

Productivity estimates diverge: about $116 billion of GDP over a decade (PC 2025), against total factor productivity gains of no more than 0.66% over ten years (Acemoglu 2024). Acemoglu also predicts a widening gap between capital and labour income, so whatever gain arrives may accrue mostly to capital. Modelled job losses concentrate in retail, public administration, finance, professional services and real estate (JSA 2025).

### 2.5 Who bears the risk

Men are 43.7% of the most-exposed group but nearly 70% of the least-exposed, and 43.7% of the most-exposed hold tertiary qualifications, against 14.9% of the least-exposed (DEWR 2026a). Women are over-represented in highly automatable roles (JSA 2025). This profile looks little like manufacturing decline, so tools designed for trade shocks need redesigning before reuse.
